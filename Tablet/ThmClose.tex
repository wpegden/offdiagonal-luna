\begin{theorem}
Let $s\to\infty$ through positive integers and let $a\geq0$ be an integer
sequence with $a=o(s)$.  In the standard eventual sense, for every
$\varepsilon>0$ and all sufficiently large indices,
\[
 r(s,s+a)\geq(1-\varepsilon)\frac{s}{e}
  2^{(s+a-1)/2-a^2/(2s)}.
\]
Equivalently, this is
\[
 r(s,s+a)\geq(1+o(1))\frac{s}{e}
 2^{(s+a-1)/2-a^2/(2s)}.
\]
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Take $s$ and $a$ along the given sequences and put $k=s+a$.  Eventually
$4\leq s\leq k$, so \noderef{F2ForwardIndependentBound} gives the explicit
digraph $D=D_2(s)$, which is loopless and $T_s$-free, on
\[
N=2^{2s-3}-2^{s-2}=(1+o(1))2^{2s-3}
\]
vertices and
\[
 \operatorname{fwi}_k(D)\leq
 2^{3s^2/2+as-5s/2+o(s)}.
\]
The $o(s)$ estimate is the separate arithmetic corollary
\noderef{F2AsymptoticCorollary}, applied to the exact rank sum in
\noderef{F2ForwardIndependentBound}.

Apply \noderef{RandomPermutationReduction}.  The resulting $K_s$-free graph
$G$ has
\[
 i_k(G)\leq(e/k)^k
 2^{3s^2/2+as-5s/2+o(s)}.
\]
If $i_k(G)=0$, then $G$ has neither a clique of size $s$ nor an
independent set of size $k$.  By the defining property of $r(s,k)$ in
\noderef{RamseyNumber}, this implies $r(s,k)\geq N$: otherwise an induced
subgraph of $G$ on $r(s,k)$ vertices would be a loopless graph of that
order with neither required witness.  This is stronger than the claimed
estimate because $N=2^{2s+o(s)}$, whereas the claimed right-hand side has
exponential part $2^{s/2+o(s)}$.  Otherwise $i_k(G)$ is a positive integer,
so choose
$p=i_k(G)^{-1/k}$.  Then $0<p\leq1$ and $p^k i_k(G)=1\leq1$; together
with $k\geq1$ and the looplessness and $K_s$-freeness supplied above, all
hypotheses of \noderef{SamplingDeletion} hold, so
\[
 r(s,k)>N i_k(G)^{-1/k}-1.
\]
Since $i_k(G)>0$, taking negative $k$-th powers in the preceding count
gives
\[
 i_k(G)^{-1/k}\geq\frac{k}{e}\,
 2^{-(3s^2/2+as-5s/2+o(s))/(s+a)}.
\]
Substitute this inequality and $N=(1+o(1))2^{2s-3}$ into the sampling
bound.  Since $k=s+a$, the exponent simplifies as
\[
 2s-3-\frac{3s^2/2+as-5s/2+o(s)}{s+a}
 =\frac{s+a-1}{2}-\frac{a^2+5a}{2(s+a)}+o(1),
\]
where $a=o(s)$ makes the discarded terms $o(1)$ after division by
$s+a$.  We compare this exponent one-sidedly with the target exponent.
For $s>0$ and $a\geq0$, the exact difference between the two main
exponents is
\[
 \left(\frac{s+a-1}{2}-\frac{a^2+5a}{2(s+a)}\right)
 -\left(\frac{s+a-1}{2}-\frac{a^2}{2s}\right)
 =\frac{a(a^2-5s)}{2s(s+a)}.
\]
If $a^2\geq5s$, this difference is nonnegative, so the exponent just
obtained is at least the target exponent.  If $a^2<5s$, its negative
part has absolute value at most
\[
 \frac{a(5s-a^2)}{2s(s+a)}
 \leq \frac{5a}{2(s+a)}=o(1),
\]
because $a=o(s)$ and $s+a\geq s$.  Including the preceding $o(1)$
arithmetic error, in either case the total exponent is at least the
target exponent minus $o(1)$, so replacing the exact exponent by the
target exponent loses only a multiplicative $2^{-o(1)}$ factor.
The factor $k/e$ is $(1+o(1))s/e$.  The main factor tends to infinity
since its exponent is $s/2+o(s)$, so the subtracted $1$ is also
negligible relative to it.  Therefore the lower bound is
\[
 r(s,k)\geq (1-o(1))\frac{s}{e}
 2^{(s+a-1)/2-a^2/(2s)}.
\]
By the definition of $r(s,k)$ in \noderef{RamseyNumber}, and since all
preceding estimates hold eventually along the given sequences, this
means that for every fixed $\varepsilon>0$ the displayed lower bound is
at least $(1-\varepsilon)$ times the target expression at all sufficiently
large indices.
\end{proof}
